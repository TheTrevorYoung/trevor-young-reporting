Three Checkpoints Between Me and the Bathroom

An early-morning okada ride across Freetown became a small window into the gray zones built into everyday systems.

By
Trevor Victor Young
Published
September 8, 2026
Original field note
January 19, 2026
Location
Freetown, Sierra Leone
Format
Field Note
Status
Published

Early one morning, somewhere between 4 and 5 a.m., I was traveling from the east end of Freetown back to the west.

I had spent the night with Fatima and my new granddaughter, and I needed to get home quickly to prepare for what was shaping up to be a long day. We had plans to work on a tractor at the National Tourism Board.

More importantly, I needed to use the bathroom. Number two. Desperately.

To move faster, I took an okada.

On that ride west, we passed no fewer than three checkpoints.

At the first checkpoint, the okada rider was stopped. He had no helmet and was wearing slippers, both clear violations. In a system that works cleanly, I would expect violations to be handled through a formal process: a ticket, a warning or whatever sanction the law provides.

Instead, there was a discussion about holding his bike, which would have meant he could not work for the rest of the day. There was an awkward pause, followed by a lecture about safety, driver responsibility and following the law.

Eventually, the rider reached into his bag, handed something over, and the checkpoint was suddenly clear.

We passed another checkpoint while the people there were occupied with other vehicles, so we continued without stopping.

At the third checkpoint, it was the same kind of delay as the first.

By then, my bathroom urgency had reached a critical level. I was seconds away from creating a public-health incident of my own. So, without waiting for the rider to act, I reached into my pocket, handed something over, and we kept moving.

That small episode stayed with me because it illustrates the gray area I keep encountering when I try to understand how systems actually work on the ground.

From a distance, life can look black and white. Laws are laws. Rules are rules. But the closer you get to real people, real urgency and real consequences, the more daily life seems to operate inside one large gray zone.

The checkpoint system, as I experienced it that morning, appeared to create its own incentives and its own consequences, especially for people simply trying to work, move around the city and get through the day.

This was not a dramatic investigation. It was one ride across Freetown, one morning, from one person’s point of view.

Except, of course, for the urgent bowel movement.

But sometimes a small experience points toward a much bigger question. This one did.
